## State of the art

There is no denying that frontier LLMs are disrupting and stirring up a lot of controversy around white collar jobs. One
of the domain that seems to receive a lot of attention is software engineering which, for a long time has been a very
appealing domain: high salaries, an abundance of opportunities, status etc. Nowadays, however these jobs are under
attack.

A few years ago in one of his talks Robert C. Martin, uncle Bob - someone I really look up to, if you don't know him
yet, look him up - stated something similar to Moore’s law:
the number of software engineers is doubling every five years, which means that at every given moment half of the
software engineers have less than 5 years of experience. I absolutely loved this, however just like Moore's actual Law,
this has followed suit - were no longer exponentially adding transistors to a chip and the number of software engineers
is no longer doubling every five years.

I read a lof of articles online and - maybe I'm biased because I am a software engineer - I keep getting a sense than
people are actually happy that software engineers roles are nose diving and that people are loosing their jobs. For some
reason non-software engineers feel like there was some kind of gate-keeping preventing them for penetrating the domain.
I am deeply saddened by this, as I am a self-taught software engineer. Most of the stuff that I learnt, and continue
learning, is via free, community based resources. And how amazing that is!
YouTube is full of resources for learning CS, architecture, etc. You just need to put it the time to do it.

## What does a software engineer do?

If you check online articles, you see a bunch of people with fancy titles: Principal Engineer, Staff Engineer, Technical
Lead, Evangelist, Architect and the list goes on. They are all some kind of software engineers. The main difference is
their experience and responsibilities. The fancier the title the more responsibility - presumably. Another term used by
people in general, often times used by managers complaining online about software engineers, is programmer and even more
condescendingly code-monkey. This comes from outsourcing shops - which are now very frowned upon, especially where I'm
from - that used to hire a lot of people that they would later rent out to clients at a premium. Whole other discussion.
Typically, programmers/code-monkeys - from what I read online - are the kind of people that expect clear defined tasks
that they just need to write the code for and claim no responsibility. The preceding is not my view on things, but a
summary of the stuff I see online.

Having clarified the above, a software engineer is typically a person with/without formal CS education that knows enough
about technology. Typically, they are flexible enough to understand business requirements that she/he can convert those
into an actual working product. As technology progressed, we've seen various flavors of software engineers: front-end,
back-end, devops, automation etc. Sometimes the business logic is so complex that there is a specialized person whose
job it is to understand it a business analyst, that writes requirements for the software engineer to implement.

Once the business is clarified the business rules are passed on to software engineers, typically in the form of tickets
or BRD - business requirement documents etc, everything place has a different name for this. This is where the
code-monkeying comes into play - according to some. The software engineer, then simply picks up the tickets and spends
an inflated amount of time implementing that in code. At the time of writing this, I have been working for 10+ years as
a "code-monkey", by some accounts and let me tell you the truth about business requirements - not hating on business
analysts, product owners or any other function.

As a software engineer, my role is to help them and the business improve operations and drive profit. My role is to
learn as much as I can from the business domain that I work in, mix that in with the technical knowledge that I have and
come up with the best solution for the problem we are trying to solve. I translate sometimes vague requirements into
instructions that the computer can understand. A requirement may say something like: An user needs to be notified when
their balance is too low. Computers - up until recently and probably not even to day - could not understand what "too
low" meant. My job as a software engineer is to negotiate with the business and understand what too low means. Computers
need a particular value. Is too low an absolute value for all users? Should it actually be based on their average
income? Should it be a percentage? This might be a trivial example that a business analyst might have though of. In that
case great. But sometimes things are pretty complicated. Sometimes for technical reasons, business functions cannot be
achieved in a particular way. It is a software engineers role, to flag this to the business, propose alternative
solutions and ensure the product servers its users. Sometimes, this is done by an architect, but as I've grown more
senior I also started doing this.
